A recurring theme in my research is that some things you would like to count are
not, in fact, constant. In a supersymmetric theory the number of _BPS states_ —
the states saturating a lower bound on their mass — is protected against small
deformations. So we form an index,

$$
\Omega(\gamma) \;=\; -\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathcal{H}_\gamma}\,(-1)^{2J_3}\,(2J_3)^2,
$$

which is insensitive to the exact spectrum and depends only on the charge
$\gamma$ and the point in moduli space. Insensitive, that is, until it isn't.

## Walls of marginal stability

The index $\Omega(\gamma)$ is _locally_ constant, but the moduli space is carved
up by real-codimension-one **walls of marginal stability**. On such a wall a
bound state of charge $\gamma = \gamma_1 + \gamma_2$ can decay into its
constituents, because the central charges align:

$$
\arg Z(\gamma_1) \;=\; \arg Z(\gamma_2).
$$

Cross the wall and the count changes discontinuously. The bookkeeping for _how_
it changes is the **wall-crossing formula**; in its primitive form the jump is
governed by the symplectic pairing $\langle \gamma_1, \gamma_2 \rangle$ of the
two charges.

## A count you can actually run

The pleasant surprise is how concrete this gets. Enumerating the stable bound
states at a point in moduli space becomes a problem about **attractor flow
trees** — rooted binary trees whose leaves are the constituent charges and whose
internal vertices sit on walls. Counting them is something a computer is happy to
do:

```python
def stable_trees(charge, modulus, *, depth=0):
    """Enumerate attractor-flow trees for `charge` stable at `modulus`."""
    trees = [Leaf(charge)] if is_elementary(charge) else []
    for g1, g2 in splittings(charge):
        if crosses_wall(g1, g2, modulus) and pairing(g1, g2) != 0:
            for left in stable_trees(g1, modulus, depth=depth + 1):
                for right in stable_trees(g2, modulus, depth=depth + 1):
                    trees.append(Node(left, right))
    return trees
```

The physics lives in `crosses_wall` and `pairing`; the recursion is just
careful bookkeeping. Run it across a family of moduli and the discontinuities
draw themselves — you watch $\Omega(\gamma)$ jump exactly as you cross each wall.

That interplay — a protected invariant, a sharp combinatorial shadow, and a
computation you can validate line by line — is most of what I find worth writing
about here.
